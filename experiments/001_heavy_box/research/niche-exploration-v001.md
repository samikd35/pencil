# Channel niche exploration — initial shortlist

Date: 9 September 2026. Status: exploratory; no niche selected. The user asked to move from the production proof to choosing a channel direction. Questions about their main objective and sustained subject interests are pending. No audience, language, revenue target or publishing cadence is assumed to be approved.

## What the proof contributes

The experiment demonstrates a recurring expressive character, readable physical action, editable motion, controlled revisions, and repeatable color/sound finishing. It does not yet establish viewer demand, sustainable episode output or channel economics. The ten-second duration and single-box scene are experimental choices, not restrictions on future topics.

The most useful creative starting point is a character encountering a problem viewers recognize, trying something, and discovering a surprising consequence. Explanations can emerge through the action. Original narration remains possible later; the temporary lack of recorded audio should not decide the permanent channel strategy.

## Shortlist

These are editorial fit judgments, not measured demand or revenue rankings.

| Direction | Specific starting audience and promise | Original example | Production and editorial tradeoff |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Work and technology comedy | People whose work is increasingly shaped by apps, automation and endless coordination; recognize the absurdity and understand the mechanism | He automated a five-minute task for three days | Strong opportunity for visual cause-and-effect; requires precise observations and accessible jokes rather than tool jargon |
| Everyday decisions and habits | People who recognize procrastination, distraction and overcomplication in themselves; see a familiar behavior clearly | He spent all day preparing to start | Strong physical metaphors and recurring props; research is needed for factual psychology claims, and the perspective must be more specific than general motivation |
| Everyday frustration comedy | Viewers wanting a short, relatable comic story about ordinary obstacles | The notification that refused to disappear | Closest to the demonstrated wordless acting; writing fresh payoffs and comic timing carries most of the value |
| Visual science and engineering | Curious viewers who want to understand a mechanism by seeing it happen | Why pushing harder does not always help | Natural animation advantage; diagrams, simulations and subject review increase work per episode |
| Money and consumer behavior | Viewers curious about the systems behind everyday purchases and recurring costs | The subscription that would not die | Prices, queues and incentives are visually explainable; accuracy and trust matter, and commercial potential cannot be inferred from a generic niche RPM claim |

History, sports and other specialist subjects remain viable if the user's interest or expertise points there. They are not excluded by the renderer or the previous proof.

## Provisional directions to test

**First candidate: work and technology comedy.** Proposed promise: “One stickman makes the frustrations of modern work visible.” The user's active interest in building AI-assisted workflows makes this worth exploring; sustained interest in covering the subject has not yet been confirmed.

Possible episodes:

- He automated a five-minute task for three days.
- The meeting that created six more meetings.
- Every productivity app wants one more notification.
- The AI assistant followed the instructions perfectly. That was the problem.
- The dashboard was green. Nothing worked.

**Second candidate: everyday decisions and habits, told through comedy.** Proposed promise: “A stickman's small mistakes reveal the patterns behind ours.”

Possible episodes:

- He spent all day preparing to start.
- The task grew every time he avoided it.
- He optimized everything except doing the work.
- One more option made the choice harder.
- He kept pushing because he had already pushed for so long.

These are original pitch hypotheses. Any explanatory claims would need topic-specific research before scripting. Titles and promises have not been audience-tested or selected by the user.

## Smallest useful selection process

1. Use the user's interests and objective to narrow to two directions. Set a starting audience, language and intended main format before committing to production.
2. Draft ten titles and three visual premises for each. Prefer the direction that produces distinctive, repeatable stories the user wants to keep making.
3. Audit comparable active channels: inspect recent uploads as well as older hits, compare similar lengths and video ages, and record premise, presentation, consistency of response, and repeated audience questions. Public observations are directional, not access to private retention or revenue data.
4. Make a matched 30–45-second concept pilot for each finalist, with comparable production effort. These private prototypes test clarity, character fit and production cost; they cannot prove YouTube demand.
5. If later authorized to publish a coherent small series, measure audience response across multiple episodes. Compare retention and average viewing, relevant comments, subscriptions/returning audience where available, and production hours. One viral result or a few friend reactions are insufficient to select a lasting niche.

Recommended next decision: pick the audience and subject the user can sustain, then choose the two candidate directions for a closer audit. No channel creation, publication, outreach or new animation has been initiated during this exploration.
