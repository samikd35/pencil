# Samtoons Script Development Brief
## Working title: Real Madrid Calls Bayern for Help...

**Document type:** Standalone creative brief for a script-generation pipeline.  
**Development stage:** Selected concept and narrowed brainstorming, not a finished script.  
**Target finished video length:** 2 minutes 30 seconds to 3 minutes 30 seconds.  
**Language:** Simple, natural English.  
**Story setting:** The 2026/27 season, using September 22, 2026 as the reference date.  
**Historical material:** Earlier seasons, especially 2024/25, and the 2020 Bayern–Barcelona 8–2 match may appear as callbacks or flashbacks.  
**Scope:** The selected concept and development points 1–8, with context, example dialogue, and factual references.

---

## Channel context

Samtoons is a YouTube channel creating expressive animated football-comedy videos. Its stories draw on real football moments and history, fictional fan claims, trolls, debates, rivalries, heartbreak, and “what if” scenarios involving teams, players, coaches, clubs, and supporters.

The objective is an entertaining, memorable, shareable football-comedy animation. It can exaggerate football events and fully embrace fan-fiction-style situations. The story does not have to behave like a documentary, but viewers should understand which historical football moments the jokes are referring to.

## Instructions for the script-generation pipeline

Develop the concept below into one connected animated story, rather than a collection of unrelated jokes. The finished script should fit the target runtime, but this brief deliberately preserves more detail and alternatives than the final video will need.

Use simple English and supply enough spoken or visual context for the jokes to make sense. Do not depend on unexplained catchphrases, compressed references, or insider slang. A viewer who recognizes the main clubs and players but does not remember every result should still understand the setup and payoff. Explain through action, props, short exchanges, and readable scoreboards whenever possible; do not repeat a visual joke in a long explanation afterward.

All dialogue in this document is **invented sample dialogue**, not a quotation from a real player, coach, or club. The emergency meeting, goal factory, phone call, characters’ fear, and supporter reactions are fictional comedy. Match results and other real-world background are separately sourced.

The main setting must remain this season. Older trolls are welcome when introduced through a photograph, brief flashback, memory, or another clear cue. Do not accidentally describe a 2020 or 2024 match as having happened this week.

Preserve the central concept and story progression. Sample lines can be polished for timing and natural delivery. Keep preferred versions separate from alternatives: the pipeline should select between competing jokes, not automatically use every option. Section 8 contains optional material, not an additional mandatory scene after the ending.

For production handoff, the eventual script should distinguish dialogue, visible action, on-screen text, sound effects, transitions, and intentional reaction pauses. Runtime must include those visual beats and pauses, not just spoken words. Source notes belong in production documentation, not in character dialogue.

## Selected concept

### One-sentence premise

Real Madrid leads an emergency meeting to find a way to stop Barcelona’s relentless scoring, but every solution makes the problem look worse—until a call to Bayern connects Madrid directly to Hansi Flick.

### The three connected story elements

**The Anti-Barça Emergency Meeting** is the main story. Clubs that have already suffered against Barcelona meet clubs worried about their upcoming matches. Madrid appoints itself the confident leader.

**Flick’s Goal Factory** is the visual world we cut away to. Barça’s players train and attack with absurd efficiency, while Flick calmly demands more goals. Their ordinary working day is everyone else’s football emergency.

**The Bayern helpline reveal** is the central payoff. Madrid calls Bayern because of its history of beating Barcelona. Bayern eventually transfers the call to the person who knows the scoring operation best: Flick himself.

### The central contrast

> **Outside Barcelona: a football emergency.**  
> **Inside Barcelona: an ordinary working day.**

Madrid tries harder and harder to appear confident. Flick barely changes his expression. Lewandowski sits between them: he understands why Madrid is worried, but Flick’s behavior does not surprise him.

### The important story logic

The joke is **not** that Madrid suddenly discovers Flick once coached Bayern. Madrid already knows that. The joke is that Madrid assumes Bayern must know how to stop something Bayern helped create.

The underlying irony is that Barcelona has hired the coach who once helped humiliate it, and other teams are now experiencing the same relentless approach from the opposite direction.

### Character functions

| Character | Fictional comic role in this story |
| --- | --- |
| Hansi Flick | The quiet, demanding supervisor. Other people celebrate the score; he notices the chances they missed. |
| Real Madrid representative | The self-appointed leader who insists it is not frightened, while its actions reveal the opposite. Use a clearly identifiable fictional club representative rather than requiring a particular real-life coach. |
| Raphinha | The fiercely committed attacker who takes Flick’s scoring targets seriously and stays involved through the phone-call payoff. |
| Lamine Yamal | The playful, inventive attacker who exploits the spaces everyone else overlooks. |
| Pedri | The calm organizer who sees the opening and makes the pass that defeats the opponents’ plan. |
| Bayern representative | Initially proud of its history against Barcelona, then uncomfortable when asked to solve the Flick problem. |
| Robert Lewandowski | The calm witness who knows both clubs and has worked with Flick. He explains the connection and punctures Bayern’s pride. |
| Other club representatives | Different responses to the same problem: fear, bargaining, resignation, and unrealistic optimism. They should not all sound alike. |
| Barça supporter | A smug observer whose own painful memories of 2020 can be exposed. |
| Barcelona goalkeeper | The unexpected final support-group member. Keep the character unnamed unless a specific current player is separately selected and checked. |
| Kylian Mbappé | Optional supporting character for the offside gag in section 8. |

## 1. Give the meeting a clear purpose

### Meeting setup

Call the meeting:

> **HOW TO STOP BARCELONA: EMERGENCY MEETING**

Inside the room are two groups: clubs that have already played Barcelona this season, and clubs worried about their upcoming matches. The first group looks exhausted. The second group is taking notes.

For current-season context, show a few results on the wall rather than have a narrator read them. Barcelona’s official results page lists the following matches: [^S1]

| Date | Result |
| --- | --- |
| September 6, 2026 | Valencia 0–5 Barcelona |
| September 16, 2026 | Barcelona 7–2 Racing de Santander |
| September 19, 2026 | Sevilla 1–3 Barcelona |

These are factual scoreboard references. The clubs’ reactions and the meeting itself are fictional exaggerations.

### Give the clubs different attitudes

Racing is horrified by seven goals. Valencia is annoyed that it did not even score. Sevilla thinks conceding only three makes it qualified to give advice.

**Possible exchange:**

> **Sevilla:** “They only scored three against us.”  
> **Madrid:** “Good. Tell us how you stopped them.”  
> **Sevilla:** “We didn’t. The match ended.”

The setup tells the audience what happened. Madrid draws the wrong conclusion. Sevilla’s answer overturns it.

### Preferred opening

Start with an opposing team celebrating wildly. Players hug, somebody falls to their knees, and the goalkeeper thanks the referee.

Then reveal the scoreboard:

> **BARCELONA 7–2 RACING**

They are celebrating because the final whistle has stopped the scoring—not because they won.

The whistle, celebration, and scoreboard should explain the joke without a second spoken explanation. Cut from their relief to the emergency meeting.

**Purpose of the opening:** Establish the exaggerated world immediately and show the damage before the meeting tries to solve it.

---

## 2. Madrid takes charge while pretending not to be worried

### Madrid’s entrance

Madrid should not enter openly admitting fear. It should enter determined to hide it.

The Madrid representative walks past everyone, takes the chairman’s seat, and announces:

> **Madrid:** “Relax. We are not here because we are scared. We are here to help you.”

While saying this, it locks the door, closes the curtains, and pushes a cupboard against the entrance.

Another club quietly asks:

> **Another club:** “Then why did you book the room?”

The dialogue claims confidence. The physical actions reveal panic.

### Madrid’s role in the story

Madrid has appointed itself the leader. Every failed suggestion therefore damages its pride. It must do more than look frightened: it proposes the plans, calls Bayern, and eventually has to admit that it belongs with the other worried clubs.

### The changing plan board

Give Madrid a board headed:

> **HOW TO BEAT BARCELONA**

As the meeting deteriorates, Madrid can become increasingly uncomfortable with that ambition. Save the decisive change to the wording for section 7.

Use consistent lettering on the board so the final visual change is immediately clear.

### Different ambitions in the room

The smaller clubs want a manageable afternoon. Madrid wants to prove it is above the problem.

**Possible exchange:**

> **Another club:** “I just don’t want to concede five.”  
> **Madrid:** “We are discussing how to win.”  
> **Another club:** “You are. I’m discussing five.”

This keeps the rivalry central while giving the other clubs their own comic objectives.

## 3. Every proposed solution takes us into Flick’s factory

### Rule for the cutaways

**The factory should answer the meeting—not interrupt it.**

Someone at the meeting proposes a solution. The next factory scene demonstrates why that solution will not work. The connection should be obvious to the viewer.

### Example: Stop Raphinha

> **Madrid:** “First, we stop Raphinha. Put everyone on him.”

Cut to a demonstration inside the factory. Several practice defenders crowd around Raphinha. He looks at them, then points to the huge space they have left elsewhere.

Pedri passes into that space. Yamal goes through.

> **Raphinha:** “Who’s watching him?”

The defenders turn. Too late.

### Why this uses the players well

Raphinha’s presence creates the problem. Pedri sees the opening. Yamal exploits it. All three players contribute instead of merely standing beside a machine or appearing in a generic scoring montage.

### What the factory looks like

Make it **a football training ground run like a factory**, not a machine that creates goals without the players.

There are football pitches, passing drills, and finishing exercises. Conveyor belts return balls, counters sit above the goals, and Flick walks around with a clipboard. The machinery exaggerates how organized and relentless the work is; the players still create and score the goals.

The factory feels completely normal to them. Outside, opponents are holding emergency meetings. Inside, Pedri is passing, Yamal is enjoying himself, and Raphinha is asking for another ball.

### Flick’s main running joke

Flick should speak less than almost everyone else. His standards are so high that a spectacular score barely satisfies him.

**Alternative exchange:**

> **Raphinha:** “Coach, we scored seven!”  
> **Flick:** “And how many did we miss?”

Raphinha’s smile disappears.

**Preferred exchange for this version:**

> **Assistant:** “We scored seven. Can we slow down now?”  
> **Flick:** “We did slow down. That is why it was only seven.”

Use one version, not both back to back. The preferred exchange explains the joke without a long speech.

### Vary the running joke visually

Flick’s next appearance can be silent. Everyone celebrates a goal, but he looks at the clock and rolls another ball onto the pitch.

This communicates the same attitude without repeating the same line.

---

## 4. Bayern becomes Madrid’s apparently brilliant solution

### The shift from tactics to an expert

After the first plan fails, Madrid stops looking for a tactical answer and starts looking for someone with experience.

> **Madrid:** “Call Bayern. They beat Barcelona eight–two. They must know something.”

### Historical context

Bayern beat Barcelona 8–2 on August 14, 2020. Flick coached Bayern, and Lewandowski played and scored in that match. This is the factual foundation for Madrid’s choice of expert. [^S2]

### Bayern’s entrance

Bayern appears on a video call, initially enjoying being treated as the powerful club with all the answers.

> **Bayern:** “Yes. Eight goals. A very good night.”  
> **Madrid:** “Good. Now tell us how to stop Flick.”

Bayern’s smile fades.

### The change in the story

Until this moment, Madrid thinks Barcelona is a problem somebody else can solve. Bayern’s hesitation suggests that the supposed expert may also be uncomfortable.

Do not make Bayern explain the entire history in one speech. Use its reaction to lead into Lewandowski’s appearance.

---

## 5. Lewandowski is the witness who knows both sides

### Character approach

Make Lewandowski the calm former employee, not another panicking victim. He has seen the system from inside. Everyone else treats the goal factory as a shocking discovery. He treats it as a familiar workplace.

### Current-season continuity

Lewandowski announced in May 2026 that he would leave Barcelona at the end of the season. For this present-day meeting, bring him onto the call as a former player; use his Bayern and Barça playing appearances in historical flashbacks. Do not describe him as retired or assume a new club is necessary to the joke. [^S3]

### Bringing him onto the call

> **Bayern:** “Robert played for both clubs. Ask him.”  
> **Madrid:** “Robert, how do we make Flick stop scoring?”  
> **Lewandowski:** “You are asking the wrong question. He spent years telling me to score more.”

### The flashback demonstrates his point

Briefly show Lewandowski in a Bayern shirt, finishing a chance. Flick immediately sends him another ball.

Then return to the Barça factory. Same action. Same calm Flick. A current Barça attacker receives the next ball in a different shirt.

> **Lewandowski:** “At Bayern, he told us to keep scoring against Barcelona. Then he went to Barcelona and told them to keep scoring against everyone else.”

The viewer understands the connection even without knowing the entire history: the coach has changed sides, but not his behavior.

These instructions and training actions are fictional dramatizations, not historical quotations.

### Give Lewandowski a joke at Bayern’s expense

Barcelona beat Bayern 4–1 on October 23, 2024. Raphinha scored a hat-trick, and Lewandowski scored Barcelona’s other goal. This supplies a historical reversal for Bayern’s proud 8–2 memory. [^S4]

During the call, Bayern proudly displays its 8–2 picture. Lewandowski notices another picture turned toward the wall.

> **Lewandowski:** “Show them the other one.”  
> **Bayern:** “That is not relevant.”  
> **Lewandowski:** “The one where Barcelona scored four against you.”

Bayern slowly turns the picture around. It shows:

> **BARCELONA 4–1 BAYERN — 2024**

The first picture should also be identifiable as a 2020 memory. These can be simple visual labels rather than spoken dates.

This makes Lewandowski more than a historical narrator. He punctures Bayern’s attempt to appear invincible.

### Where the Barça fan joke fits

A Barça supporter has sneaked into the meeting to film everybody’s panic. When Bayern shows the 4–1 picture, the supporter laughs loudly enough to reveal himself.

> **Barça supporter:** “Now you know what it feels like!”

Someone pulls an old membership card from a drawer. It has the same supporter’s face and the words:

> **FIRST MEMBER — 2020**

Behind it is a photograph of him crying beside the 8–2 score.

> **Barça supporter:** “That was before we hired their coach.”

The support group, card, supporter, and photograph are fictional props. The 8–2 result is the real reference.

This keeps Barça supporters in the joke and links their smugness to the same Bayern–Flick history. It is the preferred supporter joke because it fits the main story directly.

---

## 6. The Bayern helpline becomes the main payoff

### Madrid loses patience

By this point, Madrid has heard enough history.

> **Madrid:** “I don’t need old results. I need someone who can stop this!”

Bayern, now trying to get rid of the call, says:

> **Bayern:** “Fine. I’ll connect you to the man who knows that goal machine best.”

The wording matters. Bayern does not promise that this person will help Madrid. It simply passes the problem away.

### The transfer

> **Recorded voice:** “Please wait. Connecting you to our goal-scoring expert.”

Cut to the factory. A phone rings beside Flick.

He answers:

> **Flick:** “Hello. Hansi Flick speaking.”

Back in the meeting, nobody moves.

### Let the recognition land

Give the room a brief silent reaction. Do not shout over the reveal or immediately explain Flick’s old job. The preceding scenes have already supplied the context.

Some viewers may guess who will answer. That does not spoil the joke: the pleasure is watching Madrid realize where its brilliant rescue plan has led.

### Preferred exchange after Flick answers

> **Madrid:** “Wrong number.”  
> **Flick:** “Madrid? Good. We are getting ready for you.”

Behind Flick, Raphinha places another football into a large delivery box marked:

> **REAL MADRID**

Madrid hangs up.

The delivery box is a fictional visual threat about future goals, not a claim about a confirmed result or exact fixture date.

### Why this is the central payoff

The phone call, the factory, and the rivalry meet in one image. Madrid’s solution reaches the person causing its fear. Raphinha remains involved in the climax rather than disappearing while the club representatives talk.

---

## 7. The goalkeeper ending should complete the story

### Madrid finally drops the act

After hanging up, Madrid walks to the board.

It crosses out:

> **HOW TO BEAT BARCELONA**

And writes:

> **HOW TO SURVIVE BARCELONA**

For the first time, Madrid stops pretending that it is different from the other worried clubs.

### The unexpected arrival

Someone knocks.

A Barcelona goalkeeper enters, carrying gloves and looking exhausted. Everyone stares at the Barça badge.

> **Madrid:** “Why are you here? You play for Barcelona.”  
> **Goalkeeper:** “You only face those forwards in matches. I face them every day in training.”

The other clubs immediately make space for him.

**End on this acceptance and the room’s reaction.**

The final reversal is that being on Barcelona’s side does not protect you from the goal factory. The last joke belongs to someone the audience was not expecting.

### Plant a small clue earlier

During one factory scene, a powerful shot flies out of frame. We hear an unseen voice:

> **Unseen goalkeeper:** “Can we have a break?”

Flick rolls in another ball.

Do not show who asked. When the goalkeeper arrives at the meeting, the audience understands. Reuse the same voice for the payoff.

This makes the ending feel connected to the factory rather than attached at the last moment.

### Protect the ending

Do not add Flick opening the meeting-room door or announcing another training session after the goalkeeper’s line. The goalkeeper supplies the final joke; a second ending would weaken it.

### Practical continuity

Madrid barricades the door earlier. When the goalkeeper knocks, briefly show someone moving the obstruction or opening the door with assistance. Keep the action quick so the visual continuity supports, rather than delays, the entrance.

## 8. Two additional trolls worth keeping available

These are optional replacements or inserts. Do not automatically add both, and do not place them after the section 7 ending.

### A. Mbappé cannot escape the offside line

#### Real reference

In Barcelona’s 4–0 win at Real Madrid on October 26, 2024, Mbappé was repeatedly caught offside and had goals disallowed. Use this as a historical callback, not a claim about the present season. [^S5]

#### Give the joke context

> **Mbappé:** “We can score against them. I did! They just kept saying I was offside.”

Briefly show his old celebration interrupted by an offside flag.

Back in the meeting, he steps ahead of everyone to demonstrate his plan. The same little assistant referee appears and raises the flag again.

> **Mbappé:** “We are not even playing!”

The referee in the meeting is cartoon exaggeration. The callback supplies the context, so the gag does not depend on viewers understanding an unexplained flag when he enters a room.

#### Suggested placement

Use it during Madrid’s early attempts to look confident, before the Bayern call. It should not interrupt the historical reveal or the goalkeeper ending.

### B. Raphinha’s apology printer

#### Setup

Inside the factory, a fictional Barça fan is feeding paper into a printer. Above it is an old poster he made:

> **SELL RAPHINHA**

The printer now produces sheets saying:

> **SORRY, RAPHINHA**

Every time Raphinha scores, another page comes out. The fan reaches for a fresh pack of paper.

> **Fan:** “How many apologies does he need?”  
> **Raphinha:** “How many times did you say I was useless?”

#### Why the audience understands it

The old poster establishes that this fan wanted Raphinha gone. The goals explain the apologies. The viewer does not need to know a specific transfer rumor or social-media argument.

The poster, fan, and exchange are invented for the cartoon, not attributed to an actual supporter or post.

#### Selection preference

Keep this as an alternative to the Barça supporter’s 2020 membership-card joke in section 5. The membership-card version currently has priority because it connects directly to the Bayern story. Use the apology printer instead only if it produces a stronger script without crowding the main progression.
