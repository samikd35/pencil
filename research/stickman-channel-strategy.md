# Stickman Animation Channel Strategy

## 1. Strategic direction

**Develop football reinterpretations and educational transformations as two independent editorial propositions. Keep the same basic stick-figure production foundation, but give each proposition its own audience promise, pilot and evaluation.** The recommended first production test is football because it aligns with established subject interest and offers actions that can communicate without narration. Educational work deserves a separate test with a measurable learning outcome.

The intended audience is international and English-speaking. Main episodes should initially aim for five to seven minutes within the broader five-to-ten-minute ambition. Narration-free storytelling is the preferred format; brief English labels, original music and effects are available. These are starting choices that can change with evidence, rather than universal animation rules.

| Decision | Football reinterpretations | Educational transformations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Audience promise | Recognizable football situations become inventive visual comedy | An everyday mechanism becomes understandable through a character's experience |
| Best opening territory | Selfish players, impossible rules, transfer expectations and familiar tactical failures | Subscriptions, interface traps, incentives and simple decision systems |
| Reason to return | Recurring football situations with a new consequence | A new useful explanation in a familiar visual language |
| No-narration fit | Strong when the joke is physical and the situation familiar | Conditional: strongest when the mechanism can be demonstrated |
| Main production pressure | Ball contact, interaction between players and timing | Accurate explanation, readable labels and meaningful comparisons |
| First test | The Striker Who Refuses to Pass | One Click to Subscribe. Twelve Doors to Cancel. |

“Advanced” should mean deliberate posing, convincing contacts, varied timing, clear spatial relationships and controlled presentation. Alan Becker's public animation-principles series covers staging, anticipation, timing, arcs and overlapping action; the proposed development priorities apply those principles to simple figures.[^1]

There is evidence of audiences for animated football and sophisticated stick-figure entertainment. There is not evidence that either proposed channel will grow quickly, nor that the exact combination of basic figures and wordless football is an underserved market. The opportunity is a hypothesis to test. Existing successes support investigating the format; they do not supply a revenue forecast.[^5][^6]

**Recommended sequence:** test both promises in separate short scenes; compare comprehension and production effort; make separate long-form animatics; select one complete pilot for detailed animation. A final channel decision should follow those results.

## 3. Football: editorial territory and simple identities

**Recommended promise: familiar football problems, made physically impossible to ignore.** This gives the channel a consistent subject while allowing topical episodes, evergreen comedy and invented rules. Start with situations a football fan recognizes immediately; use the character's decision to create the next problem.

| Format | Example premise | Stick-figure treatment | Initial priority |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Player behavior | The Striker Who Refuses to Pass | An open teammate becomes increasingly impossible to overlook | First: small cast, familiar conflict |
| Absurd rule | Football, but Every Foul Makes You Smaller | A visible height marker changes after each whistle; size affects the next play | Early: one rule with escalating consequences |
| Limited superpower | The Goalkeeper Who Can Rewind Five Seconds | Repeat a recognizable action, then show the changed decision and cost | Later: continuity and repetition need careful control |
| Expectation versus reality | The Transfer Announcement vs. the First Match | Grand entrance and spotlight cut to the same figure mishandling a basic pass | Early: strong contrast, modest environment work |
| Match reinterpretation | The Space Everyone Forgot to Defend | Reconstruct the relevant arrangement, then exaggerate the neglected space | After the football motion test; requires source verification |
| Team experiment | Eleven Strikers vs. Eleven Goalkeepers | Overview establishes the teams; close shots isolate a few decisive players | Later: crowd choreography is an additional production problem |

### Recognition without detailed faces

Use a hierarchy: **team color and shirt number; a brief name label if needed; then a distinctive way of moving.** A team can share one simple body design. Number 9 might demand the ball and celebrate too early, while number 8 repeatedly checks for passing options. Identity should survive a wide shot and a small mobile screen.

For a real player, color and number alone can be ambiguous across seasons and teams. Establish the name and match context once, preserve the relevant kit era, and test recognition with people who have not read the brief. Keep fictional behavior clearly framed as an invented scenario; do not imply that a player actually said an invented line or committed misconduct.

FIFA's third-player combination analysis is a useful source for believable passing situations: the runner and the receiving player's timing matter alongside the ball carrier. Use that spatial logic as raw material for the joke.[^14] For rules-based episodes, check the relevant IFAB law and historical edition. An offside sequence must preserve the relevant pass moment and distinguish position from involvement.[^15]

The early slate should remain useful after this week's news. Add match-specific production once turnaround has been measured. A topic can attract attention while still being a poor choice if its relevance expires before the animation is ready.

## 4. Football pilot: The Striker Who Refuses to Pass

**Working length: 5:30. Main attraction: the audience sees the obvious pass before the striker does.** The basic figures carry the story through pointing, hesitation, acceleration, balance and reactions. The first version uses original player roles rather than requiring recognition of named stars.

| Time | Action and escalation | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00-0:20 | A teammate waits beside an open goal. The striker chooses an impossible solo route and loses the ball | Deliver the title's promise immediately |
| 0:20-1:00 | Establish the striker, teammate and defender. A second ignored pass produces a different failure | Make the relationships and geography clear |
| 1:00-2:00 | The striker attempts a feint, a nutmeg and a spectacular shot; the defender adapts | Escalate decisions, not just the number of failures |
| 2:00-3:00 | The teammate tries increasingly conspicuous ways to become available | Give the quieter character agency and build anticipation |
| 3:00-4:10 | A simple passing move succeeds elsewhere. The striker notices, tries to imitate it, then hesitates | Earn the change in approach |
| 4:10-5:00 | The striker finally passes, runs into space, receives the return and helps finish the move | Resolve the action with a visible cause |
| 5:00-5:30 | The striker prepares a solo celebration; the teammate redirects him into a shared one | Close with a character payoff |

These durations are a drafting budget. Shorten any repeated attempt that adds no new information. A complete three-minute version would be preferable to padding weak material to five minutes.

### The 45-second production sample

0-6 seconds: establish the open teammate, defender and goal in one readable wide shot. 6-14: striker notices the teammate, then deliberately turns away. 14-24: a feint and failed dribble show the defender's response. 24-32: hold the disappointed reaction, then reset the opportunity with changed positioning. 32-41: striker finally passes around the committed defender; teammate receives. 41-45: striker expects applause while the teammate points toward the next play.

This sample tests the hardest reusable interaction: **intentional choice plus ball possession changing between characters.** Record the contact and release frames, the ball's route, the defender's eye line and the planted foot. Check the pass in a quiet render before adding a kick transient, crowd swell or motion trail. A diagram overlay may clarify space briefly; it should not conceal an unreadable action.

The full pilot should follow only after the sample demonstrates readable interaction and the animatic sustains its escalation. An eleven-player sequence or complex stadium camera move is not necessary to answer those first questions.

## 5. Education: useful mechanisms made visible

**Recommended promise: understand one everyday system by watching a stick figure try to use it.** Begin with digital life and visible incentives. This keeps the editorial identity narrower than a channel covering any interesting fact, and it offers reusable settings such as a phone, shop, desk and simple diagram space.

| Content territory | Proposed episode | What the audience should understand |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interface choices | One Click to Subscribe. Twelve Doors to Cancel. | How an interface can make joining easy and leaving difficult |
| Hidden conditions | The Free Trial That Packed a Suitcase | How an offer changes when a deadline or recurring payment is overlooked |
| Price presentation | The Cheap Ticket That Grew at Checkout | Why the first displayed amount may not communicate the full cost |
| Competing advice | Save Every Minute. Plan Every Minute. | Why advice can point in different directions when objectives or constraints differ |
| Cooperation models | Two Stickmen. One Shared Reward. | How a stated payoff rule changes incentives inside a toy model |
| Information traps | The Message That Borrowed a Familiar Name | How a familiar-looking identity can steer attention; requires authoritative security sources before scripting |

The FTC's dark-pattern report documents difficult cancellation paths, hidden terms, misleading design and privacy choices that steer consumers. This is a concrete source base for the first group of episodes.[^13] The learning goal is to recognize a mechanism, not to portray every subscription or interface as deceptive.

### Three narration choices

**Visuals plus sparse English labels** should be the first test. Show an action, its result and a contrast. Keep important numbers and conditions on screen long enough to compare. Brief text is compatible with narration-free animation; a succession of dense text cards is a different viewing experience.

**Brief original narration** is a useful alternative when explanation depends on a definition, uncertainty or an invisible assumption. Test the same scene with and without it. Adding more diagrams and captions can consume more attention than a short spoken sentence. Brame's review supports managing visual load, using cues, segmenting ideas and matching the medium to the information; it concerns educational settings and does not establish a universal YouTube format.[^12]

**Licensed source narration** can support an adaptation of a podcast or talk, but it is an editorial choice, not a requirement. It introduces dependencies on wording, context, recording rights and tone. Reconstructing the argument in an original visual scenario can retain the source idea without requiring the source recording. Permission and YouTube's reused-content assessment remain separate.[^17][^18]

## 6. Educational pilot: the cancellation maze

**Working length: 5:30. Learning outcome: identify how an interface redirects an attempted cancellation.** Use an invented service. A stylized maze represents navigation difficulty; it is not a claim that every real service uses the same sequence or number of steps.

| Time | Screen action | Explanatory job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00-0:20 | One large button admits the figure instantly; a tiny exit opens onto many doors | Establish the contrast promised by the title |
| 0:20-1:05 | The figure tries to leave. A prominent button returns him to the starting room | Connect the physical metaphor to an interface choice |
| 1:05-2:00 | A discount detour, confusing wording and a hidden continuation each alter the route | Introduce distinct obstacles, one at a time |
| 2:00-2:50 | Pull back: the actual screens appear beside the maze, with the chosen buttons linked to doors | Explain the mechanism rather than only showing frustration |
| 2:50-3:45 | Compare with an invented clear interface offering equally visible choices and a confirmation | Show what the confusing version obscured |
| 3:45-4:45 | Present a fresh pair of screens. Hold briefly for a prediction, then demonstrate each route | Test whether the principle transfers to a new example |
| 4:45-5:30 | The figure follows the route he now understands and reaches confirmation | Finish the task and reinforce the learning outcome |

The humor belongs in the character's attempts and the exaggerated architecture. The evidence belongs in the mapping between screen choices and outcomes. Music can build frustration, then recede during the comparison. The actual mechanism must remain intelligible with the soundtrack muted.

### Source-to-scene discipline

For each factual claim, retain the original wording, source, date, relevant context and what the scene is allowed to assert. Distinguish a documented pattern from an illustrative scenario and from a joke. The FTC report supports the existence of confusing cancellation paths; it does not establish the invented service's exact interface or a current universal legal requirement.[^13]

Nicky Case's explanatory notes offer another useful practice: reveal consequential model assumptions, including how repeated interactions change the situation. A cooperation animation should state its payoff rules and avoid presenting an authored outcome as evidence about all people.[^11]

### The 45-second production sample

Show entry, one failed exit, the reset, and a side-by-side comparison with a clear exit. Limit the sample to one mechanism. Afterward, ask viewers to choose the correct button in a new layout and explain why. If they only remember a funny maze, the explanation needs revision. If they understand with sparse labels, retain the narration-free format; if not, test a brief original English explanation before adding more screen clutter.

## 7. Advanced movement with basic drawings

High-quality stick-figure motion requires a clear sequence of intention, force and consequence. Wayne Gilbert's body-mechanics instruction emphasizes what leads, what follows and why a character moves; posture contributes to the character's state.[^3] The following are proposed applications to the two pilots.

| Motion decision | Football application | Educational application |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Readable silhouette | Separate kicking leg from planted leg; show the open receiver | Separate pointing hand from a button and its label |
| Anticipation | A glance, weight shift and backswing prepare the pass | A pause and reach make a consequential click visible |
| Timing and spacing | Hold the decision; accelerate through the strike | Quick confident entry contrasts with slow uncertain exit |
| Contact | Foot meets ball before ball accelerates; receiver absorbs arrival | Finger reaches control before the interface changes |
| Balance | Support transfers into the plant and out through recovery | Lean and foot placement explain force against a door |
| Lead and follow | Hips and torso drive the action; arms help recovery | Head notices a new sign before the body redirects |
| Arcs | The foot swings through a chosen path; ball motion follows its release | Hand travels naturally toward the control |
| Reaction | Defender commits, then realizes the ball has gone elsewhere | Figure recognizes that the prominent button reset the process |
| Correction drawings | A brief altered limb shape clarifies a crossing | A clear pointing pose resolves an ambiguous overlap |
| Selective exaggeration | An overshoot sells effort without changing who has possession | An oversized doorway sells the choice without hiding its label |

The general principles are established animation practice; these football and interface choices are recommendations for this project, not published performance findings.[^1] Weight also depends on posture and the relationship between support and movement, not simply on adding a heavy impact sound.[^2]

### Rigged movement and authored corrections

Use the existing controls for movements they handle cleanly. Author the important starting, contact, passing and recovery poses; use intermediate poses to describe the path. Inspect the transitions before polishing. Automatic easing can make movement smooth while leaving the decision, balance or contact wrong.

Where a crossing or extreme pose fails, use a small editable correction drawing or change the staging. Keep the same simple proportions and line treatment. Grease Pencil's onion skinning displays adjacent keyframes to support this comparison; it is an inspection aid, not an automatic quality guarantee.[^16]

A useful contact-oriented workflow is also demonstrated in Animation Mentor's push/pull tutorial: reference and thumbnails precede detailed animation, and the mechanics are judged through blocking and polish.[^4] Transfer the principle to the existing tools rather than changing the entire production stack to match the tutorial's application.

## 8. Camera, color, sound and frame counts

### Staging and transitions

For football, begin with a view that establishes attacking direction, ball carrier, receiver and obstacle. Cut closer for the decision or contact, then return to enough space to understand the result. A top-down insert can explain a gap; the next action shot should preserve the same relationships. Limit initial scenes to the players whose choices matter, even when a wider view establishes full teams.

For education, alternate character experience with a clean explanatory view. When the interface becomes a maze, preserve the position or shape of the relevant button through the transition. Establish the connection before elaborating the metaphor. Use the same reset position for comparisons so the viewer notices the changed condition.

### Color and effects

Keep the existing character's simple outline and clear silhouette. Football can use two team colors, visible numbers and a contrasting ball. Education can assign consistent roles to actions, costs and outcomes. Pair color with labels or shapes so color is not the only carrier of meaning.

Use foreground, action plane and background to create separation. A shadow can clarify foot contact; restrained parallax can suggest depth. A brief trail can emphasize a fast ball, while a quiet hold gives the result time to register. Screen shake and glow should mark selected moments. A sequence must still read in a plain render before those effects are enabled.

### Sound without narration

Separate ambience, contact effects, character reactions and music. Football needs a discernible difference between a touch, a strike, a catch and a missed opportunity. Education needs an audible distinction between confirmation, detour and reset. Original synthesis can continue supplying simple cues; more natural crowd or Foley recordings can be added through a suitable license. YouTube's Audio Library provides music and effects, with attribution requirements on some tracks.[^21]

### Frames are not individual illustrations

| Delivery at 24 fps | Encoded video frames | What it does not specify |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 45-second sample | 1,080 | Number of drawings, poses or new assets |
| Five-minute episode | 7,200 | Amount of continuous character motion |
| Seven-minute episode | 10,080 | Production hours or quality |
| Ten-minute episode | 14,400 | Audience retention |

A reused drawing can appear in many poses through editable controls. A held pose still produces video frames. “On twos” means holding a drawing state for two frames; at 24 fps that gives 12 drawing states per second. Camera or effects can update differently. The existing project uses 24 fps; retain it for comparable tests. Higher frame rate cannot resolve weak staging or a mistimed contact.

## 9. Production strategy inside the existing project

The available foundation is stronger than a collection of concept images. Heavy Box contains an editable basic character, a ten-second motion test, a bounded revision, color and synthesized sound. Its verification records show all 240 motion frames were preserved in the effects pass. The three-minute procrastination film contains 68 held key poses and 4,320 exported frames; it demonstrates assembly and staging, not three minutes of finished continuous movement.[^23][^24]

| Stage | Deliverable | Decision it supports |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Topic and source | Audience promise, source notes, factual limits | Is there a specific joke or useful explanation? |
| Visual script | Actions, changes, framing and necessary labels | Can the central idea be communicated on screen? |
| Animatic | Whole episode at rough timing with temporary sound | Does the sequence sustain its intended length? |
| Difficult-action sample | Actual motion for the most uncertain interaction | Can the required movement be produced and revised? |
| Shot production | Editable poses, contacts, camera and selected corrections | Does each shot communicate its purpose? |
| Finish | Color, shadows, effects, sound mix and export | Does presentation strengthen the readable action? |
| Review and record | Technical checks, viewing notes, revisions and version links | What is accepted, what failed, and what can be reused? |

For the two initial niche tests, bring the difficult-action sample forward before committing to detailed full-episode work. Later episodes can order these steps around their actual uncertainty.

**Keep Blender as the animation and scene authority.** Continue using the native editable vector body and controls already present. Grease Pencil is available for additional drawn corrections and effects when a shot needs them. Existing Python sound synthesis and FFmpeg export cover the first samples. New drawing applications or a generated-video service are optional experiments, not prerequisites.

Use image generation for backgrounds, props or visual exploration where it saves work. Preserve the established stick figure. Independently generating every frame would require a separate consistency and editing test; it is not the proven workflow here. Code should specify and test motion, while rendered frames expose what that motion actually looks like.

### Reuse after evidence

Create only the moves the next shot needs. Football first needs a controlled touch, pass, receive, feint and reaction. Education first needs a reach, click, look, push and recovery. A move becomes reusable after it works in a second situation with different timing or contact. Keep episode choices in that episode's brief; share only the mechanisms that have demonstrated reuse. No new general animation framework is needed to begin.

## 10. Validation, effort and niche selection

**Compare the niches through separate tests with a comparable production allowance.** Equal length helps, but difficulty and available assets also matter. A football scene requiring several interacting figures should not be declared inferior merely because a single-character interface scene takes less time.

1. Draft three title-and-thumbnail concepts for each niche. Each should show a clear situation and a specific promise. Use new compositions rather than adding labels to the same generic character portrait.
2. Produce the separate 45-second samples described here. Record active work time by sources, staging, assets, motion, sound and revisions; record render time separately.
3. Show each sample to five to eight suitable viewers who have not read its script. For football, use football followers. For education, use interested general viewers without requiring expertise. Rotate viewing order when people see both.
4. Build separate five-to-seven-minute animatics only after the samples resolve the major movement and comprehension questions. Review pacing before finishing hundreds of seconds of animation.
5. Select one complete pilot to polish. Keep the second niche's result as an independent alternative. If both later warrant publication, preserve separate channel identities and editorial promises.

The small viewing group is a practical diagnostic, not a representative audience survey or a statistically reliable market comparison. As a working comprehension target, aim for at least four of five viewers to identify the football decision or transfer the educational principle to a new example. A missed target is a reason to inspect the scene, not an automatic rejection of the niche.

| Evaluate | Football question | Educational question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Comprehension | Who had the ball, what choice was made, and why did it fail? | What mechanism was demonstrated, and what changes in the new example? |
| Appeal | Which moment was funniest or most satisfying? | What was surprising or useful enough to remember? |
| Continuity | Was any pass, direction change or reaction confusing? | Was any comparison, label or metaphor confusing? |
| Production fit | Can contacts and player interactions be revised reliably? | Can explanations be revised without rebuilding the episode? |
| Series potential | Can ten distinct football premises be proposed? | Can ten useful mechanisms be proposed within the same audience promise? |

Estimate a pilot from its actual work categories: new assets, new motion, reused motion, explanatory layouts, sound and review. Do not multiply the first ten-second experiment's total time by thirty. Setup work may amortize; multiple actors, new environments and corrections may increase effort. A publishing cadence should follow two completed, measured episodes.

## 11. Audience development and commercial strategy

### Football

Position the first series around football behavior and impossible rules. A thumbnail for The Striker Who Refuses to Pass could show one figure surrounded by defenders while an open teammate waits beside the goal. The image should communicate the ignored option at a glance. Other testable premises include The Keeper Who Celebrates Too Early and Football, but Nobody Can Stop Running.

Prioritize evergreen episodes until turnaround supports topical commentary. Then reinterpret a timely event around one clear decision or consequence. Measure whether viewers return for the recurring football premise, rather than assuming a famous player's name will carry unrelated episodes. Potential commercial matches include football products, sports media and game-related partners; these are prospect hypotheses, not confirmed buyers. Established football-animation partnerships show that the category can support commercial activity.[^7]

### Education

Position the first series around mechanisms encountered in digital life. The cancellation-maze thumbnail can contrast one large entrance with a tiny exit inside many doors. Subsequent episodes should deliver similarly concrete insight, rather than pivoting immediately into unrelated health, finance and philosophy claims.

Potential commercial routes include commissioned product explanations, digital-literacy material and carefully matched sponsorships. Direct commissions buy a useful production result; sponsorships usually depend on the audience that result reaches. The educational option may therefore be useful as a portfolio, but there is no evidence here establishing it as the faster revenue route. Sponsor claims need the same scrutiny as editorial claims.

### Measurement and transformation

Use title, thumbnail, opening retention, average viewing and returning-viewer evidence together. YouTube's retention report distinguishes dips and spikes; a replay spike can reflect interest or confusion.[^19] Its current desktop A/B tool can test up to three titles or thumbnail combinations where eligible and evaluates results using watch time. Low exposure may leave results inconclusive.[^20]

For either niche, transform an existing source by contributing a new scenario, interpretation, comparison or explanation. YouTube distinguishes meaningful transformation from minimal changes and permits recurring formats when the substance varies. Copyright permission does not itself settle reused-content eligibility.[^17] Credit, a disclaimer or a short excerpt also does not automatically establish a copyright exception; assess the actual material and use.[^18]

Nicky Case's repository illustrates why asset checking remains specific: the project is dedicated to the public domain, but its credits list individual sound resources with different licenses, including noncommercial terms. Reusing the explanatory approach and creating original assets avoids assuming every credited component has identical permissions.[^10]

## 12. Immediate roadmap and decision record

**The next concrete deliverable should be a football interaction sample using the existing basic figure.** The selected scene must show a deliberate refusal to pass, a consequence and a readable change in possession. It should answer whether several simple figures can carry football intent and contact reliably. It is a proposed next action, not a completed production.

The independent educational sample should follow with the same production discipline: entry, one misleading exit, reset and a clear comparison. It should answer whether an audience understands the mechanism without narration. Test a short original English explanation only if the visual version leaves a specific comprehension gap.

| Milestone | Work to complete | Continue when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Establish the promise | Three distinct episode premises and packaging concepts per niche | Each promise can be explained in one sentence |
| Validate movement | One 45-second football sample and one 45-second educational sample | Important contacts and transitions read at normal speed |
| Validate comprehension | Independent viewing and a new-example question for education | Misunderstandings are identified and addressed |
| Validate duration | Separate full-length rough animatics | Each middle section develops its premise and earns its payoff |
| Choose detailed production | Select one pilot using appeal, effort, reuse and subject motivation | The decision has evidence beyond a good still or a large reference channel |
| Establish repeatability | Complete a second episode in the chosen direction | Actual production effort supports a realistic release cadence |

### Records that make a later pipeline possible

Continue the existing event journal. Record the episode and shot, source links, intended effect, chosen assets, motion version, contact frames, render settings, elapsed work, output paths and review decision when those details actually exist. Retain the difference between a technical check, an author's viewing assessment and audience feedback.

For educational scenes, also record the factual claim, supporting source, assumptions and the boundary between demonstration and evidence. For football scenes, distinguish the historical event from the invented change. Keep source-recording permissions separate from permission to reuse an idea or create an original illustration.

If later comparison becomes cumbersome, the journal's stable IDs and artifact hashes can support a small SQLite index of episodes, shots, assets, sources and reviews. The files already solve the current tracking need; building the database now would not improve the next animation.

### What remains uncertain

The existing motion test does not establish multi-player football quality, a sustainable five-minute production rate or educational learning outcomes. The preferred narration level and audience demand need direct testing. The strategy therefore retains both niches while recommending football as the first practical movement test, with education evaluated on its own explanatory merits.
